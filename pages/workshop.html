Your phone keyboard, scaled up a million times

Type "see you" on your phone and it suggests "later," "soon," or "tomorrow." It learned those habits from text people typed.

A large language model (LLM) works on the same idea. It was trained on a huge library of writing, and it learned which pieces of text tend to follow which. When you ask it something, it doesn't look up an answer. It predicts a likely next word (or word piece), adds it, and repeats.

    Myth AI thinks and understands ideas the way a person does.

    Reality An LLM generates text by predicting patterns. It can write a fluent paragraph about France without having experiences, beliefs, or a way to check facts. Fluent does not mean verified, which is why confident answers can still be wrong.

    Pick the right tool for the job

    Not every AI tool does the same thing. Two kinds are especially useful for schoolwork, and they work best together.

    Source-based assistants (like NotebookLM)

    You add your own sources, such as class readings and notes. The tool answers questions from them and points to where each answer came from.

    Academic aggregators

    Google Scholar, JSTOR, and your school library's databases help you find real papers by real authors. Use them to locate sources worth reading.

    A simple structure for prompts: PREP

    Good prompts are clear sentences, not code. PREP gives you four parts to cover. It comes from a speaking framework (Point, Reason, Example, Point) and works well for asking AI for help.

    P

    Point

    Say exactly what you need, in one clear sentence.

    R

    Reason

    Give context: your class, your level, and why you're asking.

    E

    Example

    Show what a good answer looks like, or the style you want.

    P

    Point again

    Repeat the limits: length, format, and what to leave out.

    Build a prompt

    Choose a task and fill in each part. Your prompt updates as you type. Write at least a short sentence in every part, then lock it in.

    Case study: the citation that wasn't there
    
                        
    Maya is writing a history essay about the printing press. She asks an AI chatbot for sources
                            and gets a tidy list with authors, journal names, and page numbers. She pastes three into
                            her bibliography without checking them. Her teacher searches for each one and can't find two
                            of them.
    
                        
    This has happened outside school too. In 2023, two New York lawyers were sanctioned by a
                            federal judge after a court filing cited cases that an AI chatbot had invented.

                            Talking points
                            
      
                                
    • Asking AI for ideas or leads is fine. Putting unchecked claims in your work is the
                                    problem.
      • 
                                
    • A model predicts text that looks like a citation, so a fake one has the right shape:
                                    author, journal, year, pages.
      • 
                                
    • Telling the truth early and fixing the work is almost always better than hoping
                                    nobody checks.

    Keep a human in the loop
    
                        
    
                            
    
                                
    Verify
    
                                
    Find the source yourself. If you can't find it, don't use it.
    
                            
    
                            
    
                                
    Cite
    
                                
    Cite the original source you read, and follow your teacher's rules for citing AI
                                    tools.
    
                            
    
                            
    
                                
    Disclose
